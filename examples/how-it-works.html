How silkify works

A flat picture becomes something you can press. Six ideas hold it together: a dome solved from an outline, the light of the Moon, satin that appears only where the surface moves, waves that bounce off the edge, a renderer that sleeps, and a piece of the page printed on silk.

By Russ Guo · November 2026

Left alone, the pomegranate above is a picture: the watercolour’s own colours, lit softly from the upper left. Touch it and three things happen. The surface gives under the pointer. Rings spread from the dent, reach the edge of the fruit and come back. And wherever the surface moves, it turns glossy, like satin. Leave it, and the ripples die away; within about half a minute the page has stopped drawing it at all.

None of this needs a 3D model. silkify starts from a single picture and works out the rest. This page follows it through, in the order the computer does it. Each part says first what you see, then why it looks that way; the formulas are there for anyone who wants them, and can be skipped.

01A dome from an outline

What you see

The object swells like a cushion. A round fruit becomes a ball; long, thin parts, such as a hare’s ears, stay low; and the surface is smooth everywhere, with no seam running down the middle.

Why

A picture has no depth. All silkify gets from it is an outline: the picture’s transparency, or, for a picture with no transparent background, a rectangle with slightly rounded corners. It has to invent a height for every point inside that outline. (Words work the same way: given text instead of a picture, silkify first sets them in a canvas, and the shapes of the letters become the outline.)

The obvious answer is distance: the farther a point is from the nearest edge, the higher it stands. That works for a circle, but almost nothing else. Wherever two edges are equally close, the slope changes direction all at once, and the surface folds into a ridge, like the hip of a roof. Light catches the ridge, and the silk highlight would run along it like a seam.

silkify instead treats the outline like the rim of a drum skin, and pushes the skin up from below with the same pressure everywhere. A skin under even pressure cannot fold: each point sits at the average of its neighbours, plus the same small lift. In symbols, it solves Poisson’s equation:

∇²u = −1 inside the outline, u = 0 on it; height = 2√u For a disc of radius R this gives u = (R² − r²) / 4, so the height is √(R² − r²): exactly a hemisphere.

The solve runs once, when the object is set up, and by default inside a Web Worker, so the page never waits for it. It works coarse to fine: a quick pass on a grid of up to 61 × 61 points, then 121, then 241 across (300, 150 and 100 sweeps of successive over-relaxation, a standard way to solve this equation on a grid). Two light blurs round off the staircase where the outline meets the grid; the outline itself is still cut pixel by pixel from the picture’s own transparency when it is drawn.

The Moon in the gallery skips all of this. It is already round, so it is wrapped on a real sphere.

02The light of the Moon

What you see

At rest the object looks like the picture it came from: its colours are not darkened towards the edges, and nothing about it looks like plastic. Yet light from the side still gives it a clean shadow edge.

Why

The textbook way to light a surface, Lambert’s law, makes each point as bright as it faces the light. A dome lit that way darkens steadily towards its rim. Applied to a photograph, that reads as a painted plastic ball, and the picture stops looking like itself.

Look at a full Moon, though. It is a ball, but it looks like a flat, evenly bright disc: its edge is nearly as bright as its centre. Moon dust throws light back towards where it came from, and the law that describes it, worked out by Lommel and Seeliger in the nineteenth century, depends on two angles at once: the angle to the sun, and the angle to your eye.

brightness = 2μ₀ / (μ₀ + μ), at most 1 μ₀ is how squarely the surface faces the light, μ how squarely it faces you (each the cosine of an angle).

When the light comes from roughly where you stand, μ₀ and μ are nearly equal and the brightness is close to 1 across the whole face: the picture keeps its own colours. Where the surface turns away from the light, brightness falls quickly to a crisp edge, the terminator. A floor keeps the far side from going black (0.55 of full brightness for pictures, and 0.05 by default for a sphere).

One more light gives the object volume: a broad, soft gradient that follows the pointer. It moves on a critically damped spring, so it glides to where the pointer is without overshooting, and on a desktop it drifts slowly once while the object is idle.

03Silk only where it ripples

What you see

The satin sheen appears where the surface moves and fades with the ripples. There is no line where matte picture ends and silk begins.

Why

For every pixel, silkify measures how much the ripples are moving the surface there: how far it is pushed in or out, and how steeply it slopes. That gives one number between 0 and 1. It decides how much of a satin layer and a soft highlight are mixed into the picture, and only on the side that faces the light.

silk = 5 · |height| + 26 · |slope|, at most 1 height and slope are the ripples’, not the dome’s, so they are zero at rest.

At rest that number is 0 everywhere, and every pixel is exactly the matte picture of the previous section. As the surface moves, the number rises smoothly, so the two materials blend instead of switching. The materials that are meant to shine at rest simply raise its floor: satin sets it to 0.6, foil to 1.

04Waves that bounce off the edge

What you see

Press, and rings run out from the dent, hit the outline, and come back in. The outline itself never moves.

Why

The ripples live in a small grid of numbers on the graphics card, two per cell: how high the surface is there, and how fast it is moving. Each step, every cell is pulled towards the average of its four neighbours, which is all a wave equation is on a grid; then both numbers are multiplied by 0.996, so the waves slowly lose energy. The pointer presses a dent into the grid, deeper while it is held down.

Outside the outline, both numbers are set to zero on every step. To a wave that is a wall, so it bounces back, exactly like a wave in a bathtub. The surface is then pushed along the line of sight, towards or away from you, never sideways, so the edge of the object stays put and can never fold over.

On the graphics card the grid is two small textures of half-precision numbers, one read while the other is written, swapped every step. The solver takes a fixed 60 steps a second, whatever the screen’s refresh rate, so ripples move at the same speed on a 60 Hz laptop and a 120 Hz phone; in Firefox with the display forced to 30, 60 and 120 Hz it measured 59.8, 60.7 and 59.7 steps a second. A small object gets a coarser grid, and takes several smaller steps for each of those, tuned so dents and waves keep the same size and speed on it. Twenty seconds after the last touch the waves are down to about 1% of their height, and the grid is cleared.

05Asleep when nobody touches it

What you see

Nothing, and that is the point. A page full of silkify objects costs almost nothing while nobody touches them.

Only the pointer over the object counts. A pointer moving anywhere else on the page is never passed to it, so it never wakes up: in the Lab, three seconds of moving and holding the pointer over the control panel drew 0 frames. An object scrolled out of view, or in a hidden tab, stops entirely. Two options that keep it moving, breeze and autoplay, are off by default for exactly this reason.

Off the page’s thread

By default everything heavy happens in a Web Worker on an OffscreenCanvas: loading three.js, decoding the picture, the Poisson solve, compiling the shaders, and every frame. The page itself stays free to scroll and respond. Where a browser cannot render in a worker, silkify renders on the main thread instead; without WebGL2 it draws a still frame.

It also leaves out what this picture does not need. The triangles never overlap on screen and the outline is cut per pixel, so there is no antialiasing buffer, depth buffer or stencil buffer: about 18 MB saved per object of 373 px at a pixel ratio of 2.

From 69 to 99

Nearly all of it came from one small call. Version 0.2.0 checked for WebGL2 by creating a throwaway WebGL context on the page as it loaded. On a real graphics card that costs 15 to 240 ms. On SwiftShader, the software graphics that headless Chrome uses (silkify’s own automated tests among them), it costs 2.5 seconds, all of it on the page’s own thread. Version 0.2.1 asks the canvas’s own context instead, inside the worker, before three.js is even downloaded.

Many objects on one page

Each live object holds a WebGL context, and a browser allows only about 16 of them. The gallery shows a still poster for every card, brings a card to life only when someone reaches for it, gives the context back when the card scrolls away, and keeps at most three alive. This page does the same, except that its figures come to life as you scroll to them, not when you reach for them. Live on this page right now: 0 of at most 3.

06A piece of the page, on silk

What you see

The card below is not a picture of a card. Select its words, copy them, find them with the browser’s search, follow its link: it is ordinary HTML, and it still dents and ripples when you touch it.

Why

HTML-in-Canvas is a new browser feature, available from Chrome 157: a canvas may hold real elements of the page and draw them into its own pictures. silkify first builds the dome from a stand-in of the element’s own box (its size, its rounded corners, its background colour), then prints the element over it. The element’s corners become the outline.

The element never stops being part of the page, which is why selection, links, search and screen readers keep working. The browser tells the canvas whenever the element’s appearance changes (new text, a hover, a selection, the blinking caret), and each change is one new print and one frame. From the change to the moment the new print is handed to the graphics card takes about 2 ms. When nothing changes, nothing is drawn. In a browser without the feature, or with reduced motion, the element simply stays where it was, as plain HTML, and nothing is logged.
